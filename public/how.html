How it works

A summary you can audit, one sentence at a time.

A summary can sound right and still be wrong: the wrong speaker, a dropped "maybe", a number that was never said. Gavel does not grade how a summary sounds. It checks whether each sentence is actually in the recording, and shows you the passage.

1

Transcript

Captions are fetched from YouTube (or pasted) and cut into short passages of 20 to 45 seconds, ending at sentence boundaries.

2

Claims

A model writes 6 to 20 standalone sentences that cover the whole recording, keeping names, numbers and hedges exactly as spoken.

3

Evidence

For each claim, a search over the passages picks the five most likely sources. Only those five are shown to the judge.

4

Verdict

A judge model reads the claim and its five passages and answers: supported, partly, not supported, or contradicted, plus who said it and a quote.

5

Flag

A fixed rule decides which verdicts to flag. You can see the trade-off on the toggle: the rule matters more than the model.

The threshold matters more than the model

The judge gives every claim one of four verdicts. Which of those you treat as a flag is a choice, and it changes the numbers more than switching models did. Both settings below use the same verdicts, measured on the 1,064-claim benchmark.

Default: flag only clear problems

Flags "not supported", "contradicted", and "wrong speaker". Almost never flags a correct claim.

If you are skimming a lecture, use the default: anything flagged is worth a look. If you are checking a summary before you rely on it, turn the toggle on and read the partly-supported ones yourself.

Choices that came from the benchmark

Gavel Live is the product end of a benchmark that was built first. A few decisions only make sense in that light.

Keyword search, no embeddings

The evidence search is BM25, a classic keyword ranker, and nothing else. In the benchmark, adding a dense vector search did not improve results (F1 0.83 vs 0.81), so there is no vector index to run or pay for.

A forgiving parser

The first version of the benchmark silently threw away 75% of the judge's answers because of a formatting quirk, and defaulted their severity. The parser now repairs and salvages responses, and every verdict records whether it needed repair. You see that count on each result.

The judge only sees five passages

That keeps verdicts cheap and traceable: every "why" points at a passage you can play. It also means the judge cannot see what a summary left out. Omissions are the next thing to measure.

What it cannot do yet

Speakers

Auto-generated captions have no speaker names, so "who said it" is only checked when the transcript names them (pasted transcripts can).

Omissions

A claim can only be judged if it exists. If the report never mentions something important, nothing flags that. A coverage check is planned.

Not advice

Verdicts are about whether the recording supports a sentence, not whether the sentence is true in the world. Not medical, legal or financial advice.
